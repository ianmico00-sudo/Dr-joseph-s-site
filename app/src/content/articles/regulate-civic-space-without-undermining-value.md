It is often argued by some decision makers that unregulated civic spaces give rise to "briefcase NGOs" entities that emerge when funding is available, lack governance structures, and deliver limited impact for citizens. While this concern is not entirely unfounded, it presents only a partial reading of Rwanda's civil society trajectory and risks overlooking the historical, political, and developmental context in which civil society organizations (CSOs) emerged and evolved. A more balanced assessment shows that civil society in Rwanda has largely been a partner in reconstruction, stabilization, and development, not a destabilizing force. Where challenges have arisen, they are better understood as systemic and transitional, rather than evidence that civil society itself lacks legitimacy or value.

Civil society after 1994, necessity, not opportunism

Following the 1994 Genocide against the Tutsi, Rwanda was a country in near total collapse. State institutions were weak, social trust was shattered, millions were displaced, and trauma was widespread. In this context, civil society organizations played an indispensable role in humanitarian response, reconciliation, public health, and poverty alleviation. Local CSOs partnered closely with government institutions and international agencies to address urgent needs ranging from shelter and food security to trauma counselling and community sensitization. Importantly, these organizations were not driven by external ideological agendas. Had they been, the recovery process would likely have been chaotic and socially destabilizing, given the level of trauma and insecurity at the time, including the presence of ex-FAR and Interahamwe forces across Rwanda's borders attempting to regroup and destabilize the new state led by the Rwandan Patriotic Front. At that historical moment, there was no luxury for prolonged experimentation or adversarial policy testing. National survival required collective discipline, trust-building, and home-grown solutions tailored to Rwanda's unique context.

Home-Grown Solutions and the role of CSO's

Civil society organizations were instrumental in supporting and popularizing Rwanda's unconventional but effective home-grown solutions, including community-based justice, reconciliation initiatives, and collective development approaches. Through sustained grassroots engagement, CSOs helped citizens understand and adhere to these frameworks, reinforcing national cohesion. It must be acknowledged, however, that a small number of CSO leaders adopted confrontational or ideologically rigid approaches that underestimated the fragility of the post-genocide context. In some cases, critical examination of policies was pursued without sufficient appreciation of the existential threats facing the state at the time. This was less a matter of malice and more a failure to fully "read the room," a mismatch between advocacy frameworks and national realities.

From humanitarian action to development planning (2003–2012)

By the early 2000s, Rwanda had transitioned from emergency response to long-term development planning. Most CSOs until 2003 remained focused on humanitarian relief and poverty reduction. The launch of Vision 2020 marked a turning point, as the government embarked on an ambitious agenda to transform Rwanda into a middle-income country.

During this period, CSOs were increasingly invited to participate in Technical Working Groups across ministries and public institutions. Those with strong leadership, technical capacity, and institutional discipline contributed meaningfully to policy discussions. Notably, many effective CSO leaders had passed through RPF cadreship programs, which emphasized values such as consultation, discipline, and collective responsibility encapsulated in the principle of "Kugisha inama no kugirwa inama" (consulting and being consulted). A notable example is the cohort of feminist leaders who participated in the 1995–1996 Beijing World Conference on Women. This generation played a pivotal role in advancing gender equality reforms that today position Rwanda as a global leader in women's political representation. Many of these leaders later served in senior government roles, not because of opportunism, but because of demonstrated competence and shared national values.

The HIV/AIDS era and the expansion of NGOs

The mid-2000s saw a rapid expansion of NGOs, particularly in the health sector. This growth coincided with major global health financing initiatives such as PEPFAR, the Global Fund, UNAIDS, and the World Bank. Rwanda, like many countries in the region, faced an acute HIV, TB, and malaria burden, coupled with severe human resource constraints in the health sector. NGOs emerged not simply because funding was available, but because the scale of the pandemic demanded mass awareness, community mobilization, and decentralized service delivery functions that NGOs are uniquely positioned to perform. This is evidenced by what happened later: as HIV programming was progressively transitioned to government systems, many of these NGOs naturally closed or merged, having fulfilled their mission.

Regulation and the 2012 NGO Law

In 2012, Rwanda adopted a new NGO law through a highly participatory process involving government, civil society, religious leaders, and development partners. At the time of its passage, the law was widely regarded as one of the most enabling regulatory frameworks in the region. It required only 3 founding members to register an NGO, a collaboration letter from the district, and proof of an office address and governance structures. These were reasonable safeguards, not barriers. The law recognized a practical reality: funding cannot precede legality. Organizations needed a legal identity before mobilizing resources. There was little controversy at the time, and no significant objections were raised after its adoption.

Shifting perceptions and the 2015–2024 period

Tensions began to emerge around the 2015 constitutional revision process. A perception took hold among some actors that CSOs were being funded to oppose the referendum. Although many CSOs ultimately participated constructively, the narrative of "donor-driven activism" had already begun to circulate. This period also exposed internal weaknesses within the sector. Competition for funding intensified, peer reporting increased, and a few individuals were labelled as "donor darlings." Some decision makers exploited these dynamics to discredit alternative perspectives and hiding their own weaknesses rather than engage them substantively. A critical weakness that has undermined civil society's positioning is its failure to establish its own niche independent of government implementation.

Rather than developing distinct agendas rooted in grassroots constituencies, many CSOs have been perceived as implementing partners for government priorities. This identity crisis has made civil society vulnerable to dismissal as mere instruments of state policy rather than independent voices representing citizen interests and concerns. The 2024 NGO law was again developed through minimal consultation. While some improvements were made, a few provisions have continued to fuel perceptions that Rwanda's civic space is restrictive, particularly in international discourse, even at the recent UPR review mechanism in Geneva this month. Several provisions in the new law illustrate the tightening of civic space. These include provisions governing NGO registration timelines, restrictions on advocacy activities, requirements for the approval of the NGO leadership structure, and limitations on organizational autonomy in priority-setting. Such articles, while framed as regulatory improvements, have been interpreted by many actors in civil society as constraining the operational freedom organizations need to function as genuine accountability actors.

Funding realities: What is the missing pillar?

Globally, civil society is funded by governments, private sector actors, and philanthropy. In many Western countries, governments are major funders of their own CSOs, which come to Africa and fund local CSOs to implement on their behalf. In Africa, this is rarely the case. Governments often expect CSOs to supplement state efforts, yet provide little direct financial support. In Rwanda, private sector engagement with CSOs has historically been limited, with most support directed toward government initiatives. Only recently, during revisions to the tax law at the parliament level, did the private sector begin to formally recognize CSOs, following sustained advocacy and parliamentary engagement by civil society actors.

Political will exists, implementation gaps remain

There is no shortage of political will at the highest level. On 13th September 2016, a Cabinet meeting chaired by H.E. the President approved a framework for CSO collaboration in implementing pro-poor government programs aimed at improving citizens' livelihoods. The initiative was promising but faltered due to bureaucratic inertia, excessive meetings, and a lack of institutional ownership. Beyond the absence of political will at the implementation level, a more troubling dynamic exists: some government leaders actively avoid being informed on policy challenges and governance failures. This reluctance stems not from ignorance of problems, but from fear of accountability. When civil society attempts to conduct evidence-based research on critical issues, many government institutions respond with silence, refusing to complete questionnaires or participate in research processes. This institutional resistance, grounded in fear of accountability mechanisms, prevents civil society from accessing the data necessary to develop research-backed solutions.

Beyond false dichotomies

The perception that NGOs "emerge when funding is available, lack governance, and have little impact" is an oversimplification. Rwanda's civil society has contributed meaningfully to humanitarian relief, health outcomes, gender equality, poverty reduction, and policy development, among other areas. The way forward lies not in counter-accusations, but in stronger systems for reporting, feedback, co-financing, and mutual accountability. Civil society is not an adversary of the state; it is the third pillar of society, alongside government and the private sector. As leadership at the Rwanda Governance Board evolves, there is hope that collaboration will deepen, trust will be rebuilt, and Rwanda will move closer to a mature, confident civic ecosystem. As the proverb reminds us, "Those who come together, God joins them."
