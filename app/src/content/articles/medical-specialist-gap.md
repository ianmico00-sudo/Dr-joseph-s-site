About a month ago, a young woman called me with a difficult request. She asked whether I could help her secure an earlier appointment at RMH to see an orthopaedic specialist. Years earlier, she had been involved in a serious accident that injured her hip. Over time, the pain had worsened. By the time she called me, it had become unbearable. She could barely walk, which meant she could not work. Which meant she could not provide for her family, despite being the breadwinner. What made the situation even more distressing was that she had already been given an appointment four months later. Four months for someone who could not walk, could not earn, and could not wait. She was not asking for special treatment. She was asking for access to care when it still mattered.

That call immediately took me back to an experience I had in 2022, three years ago, when I was seeking oncology care for my late father at RMH, one of the country's major referral hospitals. I arrived before 9:00 a.m., believing I was early. I was wrong, the waiting area was already full. Some patients had arrived at dawn. Others told me they had been coming repeatedly for weeks sometimes for over a month without successfully securing an appointment. The consultation area was overcrowded and noisy. Patients, caregivers, fear, frustration, and exhaustion filled the room. It felt less like a specialized referral service and more like a congested holding space. That moment stayed with me, not only as a son navigating illness and loss, but as a citizen observing how our health system handles its most complex cases. I left without seeing the oncologist because I also sympathized with him due to the number of patients waiting. It only requires a selfless human being but the demand is high.

Taken together, these two experiences years apart, involving different hospitals and different specialties point to a reality we must confront honestly; we have a serious shortage of medical specialists, while demand for specialist care continues to rise.

Rwanda's health infrastructure looks strong. The country has built over 1,000 health posts, more than 500 health centres, around 45–50 district hospitals, and six national referral hospitals, including King Faisal Hospital Rwanda and the University Teaching Hospitals in Kigali and Huye. This expansion has been critical in improving access to primary care, maternal health, and basic services. Buildings do not treat patients. People do, when it comes to specialists' orthopaedic surgeons, oncologists, cardiologists, neurologists, nephrologists the gap is increasingly visible. Patients now wait months to see specialists. Conditions that could be managed early become disabling. Breadwinners fall out of the workforce. Families sink deeper into vulnerability. The cost is not only medical; it is economic and social. At that point, a practical question formed in my mind not as an accusation, but as a serious policy and business reflection: what would it actually take to deliberately recruit specialist doctors from the region?

Why can Rwanda not systematically attract orthopaedic surgeons, oncologists, anaesthesiologists, and other specialists from Zimbabwe, Burundi, Sudan, Ethiopia, or the wider Horn of Africa? These countries have trained competent doctors, many of whom face limited opportunities, poor working conditions, and instability at home. We often hear the argument that "we cannot afford them." But do we ever calculate the cost of untreated disease, prolonged disability, lost productivity, and families pushed into poverty?

Another unavoidable question follows; what happened to the doctors trained under the Human Resources for Health (HRH) Program? This program was visionary. It strengthened teaching hospitals, upgraded medical education, and produced Rwandan specialists. Yet clearly, the scale of output has not matched population growth, epidemiological transition, and rising demand. Either the numbers remain too small, retention is weak, deployment is uneven, or specialists are overwhelmed by systemic inefficiencies.

Governance must also be part of this conversation. Most referral and district hospitals in Rwanda remain fully public, with decision-making centralized under the Ministry of Health. Centralization has benefits coordination, equity, standardization but it also creates rigidity. It slows adaptation, limits managerial autonomy, and weakens accountability at the point of service delivery. One day I visited Rwamagana provincial hospital and I saw more than 6 cars including ambulances backed in the compound waiting for the approval for maintenance from Kigali yet the hospital has a procurement officer and a fleet manager. Why can there not be serious public private partnerships, especially in specialist care, diagnostics, and hospital operations? Why can districts not own and manage their district hospitals with greater autonomy, then bill the Ministry of Health based on services delivered and outcomes achieved?

If decentralization is to mean anything beyond administration, it must extend to service delivery. If we want real decentralization this will require something uncomfortable but necessary: we must be serious about the quality of district leadership. District councils are not ceremonial. If districts are to manage hospitals, then performance, competence, and integrity must matter when choosing or electing local leaders. The good news is that in November 2026 we are going to elect new local leaders. So, the ball is in our hands!

Let us look at very recent, concrete examples. Just yesterday, a friend took her 82-year-old mother to RMH for a medical check-up. After waiting for three hours, they left without laboratory tests being conducted. No results. No clarity. Just exhaustion. Yet she had been advised to change medication for her mother. But because she is educated, she wanted to first see the results before switching her to a new line of treatment.

On a personal level, I have lived with hypertension for several years. Recently, I went for routine tests at King Faisal Hospital Rwanda. For two weeks, I could not access my laboratory results, not even my ECG report. I went to Lancet and paid out of pocket. I requested the same tests and got the results in less than 45 minutes via both my phone number and email. Yet when one visits private facilities such as Lancet Laboratories Rwanda, The Frontier Diagnostic, Baho Hospital, or Legacy Clinics, results are often delivered electronically within 30 minutes except for tests that genuinely require culture or advanced analysis, sometimes sent to Nairobi.

This raises a fundamental systems question: how does a small private laboratory deliver faster results than a district or referral hospital that is publicly funded and better equipped? The answer is not technology alone. It is systems, incentives, workflow design, accountability, and institutional culture. We are often told correctly that public hospitals are overwhelmed with patients. But then we must ask the harder question: why are they overwhelmed despite the scale of our health infrastructure? Why do health centres and district hospitals fail to absorb and resolve cases effectively? Why do patients bypass lower levels of care? Why do referral hospitals become congestion points rather than centres of specialist excellence?

This is not an attack on healthcare workers. Many are overstretched, underpaid, and doing heroic work under pressure. This is a structural problem. Rwanda is changing. Life expectancy is increasing. Non-communicable diseases are rising. Road traffic injuries are more common. Cancer detection is improving. These trends demand a different level of specialist capacity and operational efficiency. We can be grateful for the gains Rwanda has made in community-based health insurance, maternal health, and primary care. But gratitude should not require silence. Progress depends on honest reflection.

We are fortunate to have leadership that consistently emphasizes citizen-centered service delivery. But leadership alone is not enough. Systems improve only when citizens, professionals, districts, and policymakers are willing to confront uncomfortable truths.

I will not offer recommendations today. Sometimes, the most responsible contribution is honesty, naming the gaps, questioning assumptions, and admitting that something in the system is not working as intended. Only when we look ourselves in the mirror and tell the truth calmly and courageously can we contribute meaningfully to Vision 2050, not as slogans, but as lived reality. This is not criticism for its own sake; it is my civic responsibility!
