When I was about eleven, my father, may his soul rest in peace, bought me a copy of Cry the Beloved Country, a novel by Alan Paton. At the time, I could not fully grasp its depth, but with the years, its message has stayed with me. Published in 1948, the book offered more than a story; it was a window into the moral and social fractures of South Africa under apartheid. It quietly but powerfully drew attention to the injustice of that system and the human cost behind it.

Across the African continent, there was a shared sense of solidarity during those years. Many countries, communities, and individuals supported the struggle led by the African National Congress in different ways. That history makes what we are witnessing today all the more difficult to understand. The recurring scenes of African migrants being targeted, attacked, or pushed out of communities in South Africa feel deeply contradictory to that legacy of unity.

To be clear, no country is expected to ignore its immigration laws. Every state has a responsibility to regulate entry and residence within its borders. But the manner in which this is sometimes being done is marked by violence, hostility, and dehumanization raises serious moral and social concerns.

What is unfolding in South Africa today is painful, but it is not random. It is rooted in a history that left deep structural and psychological scars. Apartheid did not simply separate people; it systematically excluded the majority of Black South Africans from quality education, economic participation, and basic dignity. By the time the system formally ended in 1994, political freedom had been achieved, but millions were entering a new era without the tools or opportunities needed to compete and thrive in the economy.

The transition led by the African National Congress was historic, but the scale of what needed to be rebuilt was immense. Decades later, the country still grapples with high unemployment particularly among young people and persistent inequality. In such an environment, frustration can easily build. Unfortunately, that frustration is sometimes directed toward foreign African nationals, who become visible but unfair targets.

This is not a question of individual shortcomings. It is about the long-term consequences of systemic exclusion. Still, one important reflection remains: beyond economic reforms, was enough done to deliberately rebuild social cohesion, restore confidence, and shape a shared national identity in the post-apartheid period? Policies like Black Economic Empowerment were necessary, but they could not fully address the deeper social and psychological fractures left behind.

In this context, the experience of Rwanda offers an interesting point of comparison. After the 1994 Genocide against the Tutsi, the country faced a near-total collapse of its social fabric. The response that followed, led by the Rwandan Patriotic Front, went beyond rebuilding infrastructure or restoring governance. It placed strong emphasis on rebuilding people's sense of identity, trust, and shared purpose.

Initiatives such as Ingando, implemented through the National Unity and Reconciliation Commission, were designed as structured platforms for civic education and dialogue. They brought together citizens from different backgrounds to reflect on history, confront the consequences of division, and internalize a collective vision for the future. Over time, such efforts contributed to restoring dignity, rebuilding confidence, and strengthening a sense of national belonging.

What stands out in this approach is its deliberate nature. It recognized that rebuilding a nation is not only about economic recovery or political stability. It is also about reshaping how people think, how they relate to one another, and how they imagine their future. While some observers have described such efforts as "social engineering," they can also be understood as purposeful nation-building structured, long-term, and centered on social cohesion.

This is not to suggest that one country's model can be directly applied to another. Context always matters. But it does highlight an important lesson: sustainable transformation requires more than economic policy. It demands intentional investment in human capital, social trust, and a unifying national identity.

Perhaps the deeper takeaway is this: political liberation is only the first step. Without parallel efforts to restore dignity, rebuild confidence, and strengthen cohesion, the legacy of past injustices can persist, sometimes emerging in ways that harm others.

Societies that recognize this early, and act with intention, are better positioned to turn painful histories into foundations for unity, resilience, and shared progress.
