I have sat in spaces where reports about young people abusing drugs are shared with real concern. I have also been in discussions where leaders reflect on the future of the Rwandan family, often reminding us that the family remains the foundation of sustainable development. Listening to these conversations, I find myself asking a simple but uncomfortable question; where could things have gone wrong? At what stage did we begin to lose some of the moral grounding that shapes responsible citizens?

We often speak about the behaviour of young people as if it begins in adolescence, yet values are formed much earlier. Between the ages of 1-7 children spend most of their time with parents or guardians. This is the stage where discipline, empathy, respect and responsibility are introduced, not through lectures, but through daily example. Children observe how adults speak, how they solve problems, how they treat others, and what they tolerate. These early years quietly shape how a child understands right and wrong.

Between 7 and 12 years, other influences begin to play a role. Schools, churches and the wider community start reinforcing certain norms. In Rwanda, primary schools have traditionally emphasized discipline, punctuality, cleanliness and respect for others. Religious institutions often reinforce values such as honesty, compassion, and self-control. Community practices like Umuganda help children understand that society functions better when people contribute to the common good. At this stage, children start understanding that they are part of something bigger than their household.

From 13 to 18 years, peers become more influential during this period young people spend more time with friends and the desire to belong becomes stronger. In places like Kigali, social media and changing lifestyles expose adolescents to different ideas and pressures. Some influences are positive, others less so. Programs such as Itorero, youth initiatives, and school clubs try to reinforce good citizenship, but their work becomes easier when a strong foundation was already laid earlier in life. When the moral foundation between 1 and 7 years is weak, it becomes harder to correct behaviour later. By adolescence, habits and attitudes are more established. This does not mean change is impossible, but it requires more effort from families, schools, and institutions. It reminds us of those conversations about youth behaviour should not only happen when challenges appear, but much earlier, within the family and community.

Perhaps instead of blaming young people alone, we should reflect on the wider environment that shapes them. Families, schools, faith institutions, communities and public policies all play a role in shaping responsible citizens. Rwanda's progress has always depended not only on economic growth, but also on strong social values that promote trust, responsibility, and mutual respect. We also need to accept that raising responsible young people is a shared responsibility. As a society we should be open about the challenges we see and honest about what needs to improve. Sometimes we hesitate to tell one another uncomfortable truths, even when silence allows problems to grow. Being able to speak honestly to a neighbour, a friend, or a fellow parent should not be seen as interference, but as care for the wellbeing of our children and our country. When communities are willing to guide, correct and support one another, we create an environment where young people can grow with clearer direction and stronger values.
