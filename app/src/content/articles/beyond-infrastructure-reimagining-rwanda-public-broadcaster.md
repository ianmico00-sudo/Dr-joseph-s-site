A strong public broadcaster is an essential pillar in the development of any modern society. Beyond simply delivering news and entertainment, a public broadcaster plays a strategic role in shaping national dialogue, strengthening civic education, and promoting values that support social cohesion and national development. In many countries, public broadcasters are responsible for ensuring that citizens across both urban and rural areas have access to reliable information, educational programming, and platforms that reflect national priorities and cultural identity.

In Rwanda, the Rwanda Broadcasting Agency has historically played this role through its flagship outlets such as Radiorwanda (RBA) and RwandaTV, alongside other television stations and a network of community radio stations serving audiences across the country. These platforms have been central in informing citizens about government policies, promoting national unity, and providing educational programming that supports Rwanda's development agenda. It is therefore encouraging to learn from an article published by ktpressrwanda that the government plans to significantly increase funding for RBA in the 2025/2026 fiscal year.

The article further explains that according to the approved 2025/2026 national budget, the agency will receive Rwf 4.71 billion, up from Rwf 2.52 billion in the 2024/2025 budget, an increase of about 87 percent. This increase signals a strong commitment by the government to strengthen the country's broadcasting capacity. According to KT Press, the increase in funding is largely directed toward capital investment, reflecting a significant investment in capital spending on broadcasting infrastructure and ICT equipment.

Indeed, modern broadcasting infrastructure is critical in today's fast-changing media landscape. KT Press reports that the funds will support upgrades in studios, transmission systems, and digital broadcasting technology. However, while this investment is welcome, it also opens an important conversation about the broader transformation needed within the national broadcaster. One notable issue is the absence of any mention of staff retention and professional development.

Over the years, RBA has increasingly become known to some as "a training ground" for journalists who later transition into communications and public relations positions in government institutions, international organizations, and private companies. Many talented reporters and presenters begin their careers at RBA, build strong professional profiles, and are later recruited by institutions that offer significantly better salaries and working conditions.

This pattern has created a situation where the public broadcaster continuously trains talented journalists but struggles to retain them once they reach a certain level of experience. While career mobility is natural in any profession, the loss of experienced journalists weakens institutional memory and affects the quality and consistency of programming. If the government is investing billions of francs in broadcasting infrastructure, it would be equally important to invest in the human capital that drives content production. Modern studios alone cannot produce meaningful journalism without experienced producers, editors, digital storytellers, and investigative reporters.

Another issue that deserves attention is the pace of digital transformation. Rwanda has made impressive progress in expanding digital connectivity, with smartphones becoming widely accessible across the country. Young people in cities like Kigali increasingly consume media through their phones rather than traditional television sets. Yet many RBA programs still rely heavily on conventional broadcasting formats that require viewers to sit in front of a television at a specific time. In a world where audiences expect on-demand access to content through digital platforms, this model risks becoming outdated.

Interestingly, some of the most popular digital content today is produced by independent creators using minimal equipment. Social media personalities and street-level content producers often reach thousands of viewers through platforms such as YouTube, TikTok, and Instagram. Meanwhile, RBA possesses some of the best studio facilities in the country but does not always fully utilize them to produce dynamic digital content. Another policy question concerns the role of advertising in public broadcasting. According to the KT Press article, in addition to funding from the national budget, the state broadcaster also participates in the country's advertising market, competing directly with private media houses for commercial revenue.

While this provides an additional revenue stream for RBA, it also creates competition with private media outlets that depend heavily on advertising to survive. One could therefore argue that the national broadcaster should focus primarily on its public service mission. Rather than competing aggressively for advertising revenue, RBA could concentrate on producing high-quality content that promotes development, civic education, and positive social behaviour.

This would align well with some of the recommendations discussed during the National Umushyikirano Council, where behavioural change among citizens has been repeatedly highlighted as a national priority. Public broadcasting has the potential to play a major role in shaping attitudes around public health, financial responsibility, environmental protection, ethics, values and civic engagement. The KT Press article itself concludes that the funding increase could represent a major turning point, enabling long-delayed upgrades that may improve production quality, expand coverage and strengthen the reach of state media across the country.

Indeed, this moment presents a valuable opportunity. With increased funding, RBA has the chance not only to modernize its infrastructure but also to rethink its strategic direction in the digital era. Rwanda already has a digitally connected population. Citizens across the country carry smartphones and access online platforms daily. Yet a large portion of the content they consume comes from informal online creators rather than national public media. For RBA to remain relevant, it must move decisively toward digital broadcasting, on-demand content, podcasts, and mobile-friendly programming. By investing in journalists, embracing digital innovation, and focusing on impactful content, the national broadcaster can strengthen its role as a driver of national dialogue and development communication in Rwanda's rapidly evolving media landscape.
