In Rwanda, the narrative around electricity has never been about being the cheapest in the region; our utility tariffs are among the highest in Africa because the strategic promise was never low-cost, it was universal availability. High tariffs are essentially a contract between the citizen and the state. In exchange for paying some of the highest per-kWh rates on the continent, the expectation is an industrial-grade supply that remains stable regardless of the season. However, as consistent power cuts resurface during the dry months, a critical question arises, if households and businesses are paying a premium for energy security, are they actually getting their value's worth?

The recurring seasonal outages suggest a fragility in the grid that climate variability can no longer excuse. While the government's rapid expansion of the last mile is a significant achievement, electricity is only truly available if it is reliable. To bridge this gap, Rwanda's investment strategy must move beyond a heavy reliance on weather-dependent sources and embrace a more pragmatic, diversified energy portfolio. This requires maintaining robust firm power reserves non-intermittent sources that act as a necessary cushion. In a premium-price market, utilizing diversified generation to prevent a blackout isn't a policy failure; it is a service requirement for a population already paying for top-tier access.

This need for reliability extends beyond the national grid and into our own neighbourhoods. During a recent forty-five-minute outage in Kicukiro, I watched businesses grind to a halt because they lacked any form of transition system. It is a striking contradiction that even in neighbourhoods where homes are valued at $400,000, private energy infrastructure remains a rarity. This isn't just a lack of mindset; it is a failure to recognize the value of distributed energy as a critical backup and redundancy system rather than just an off-grid alternative for rural areas.

The bottleneck to this resilience often lies in regulation. Currently, the National Energy Policy places limitations on privately owned renewable systems, particularly when they reach a certain scale. If the national goal is total energy security, we should be encouraging, not restricting, the ability of estates, apartment blocks, or cooperatives to generate their own power. By allowing urban clusters to invest in solar as a secondary fail-safe, we reduce the strain on the national grid and ensure that the rhythm of everyday life isn't interrupted when water levels at the dams drop.

Ultimately, we must stop treating electricity as an abstract policy goal and start treating it as a purchased service. The conversation should no longer be about the difficulties of managing a grid; it should be about whether the premium being charged is delivering the promised results. The seasonal excuse for power cuts is no longer acceptable in a high-tariff environment. To truly secure our energy future, we must diversify our generation sources, strengthen our reserve capacity, and deregulate private backup systems. Energy security shouldn't just start at the dam; it must be guaranteed at the socket.
