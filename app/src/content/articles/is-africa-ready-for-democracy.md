Democracy represents freedom, choice, and oneness, a government of the people, made by the people and for the people. For many continents including Africa, democracy meant an end to subjection of military rule, colonial control, apartheid and authoritarianism among so many ruling systems that were deemed unwanted and so totalitarian by the civilians. However, there has been a lingering question of whether Africa is ready for democracy from both foreigners and Africans themselves. When the French President, Jacque Chirac allegedly said that "Africa is not ready for democracy," some Africans wanted his head. They wanted him crucified for what he said, they wanted him to withdraw the statement and even apologize for making what they termed as "a demeaning statement." But many scholars and political analysts have still asked the million-dollar question, is Africa truly ready for democracy, well that is not my question to answer, rather one for Africans to contemplate.

Looking at the recent trends in the change of power; Zimbabwe's former President Robert Mugabe, the man who was called a war hero for leading independence struggles in Zimbabwe and bringing about an end to the white-minority rule, was the same man who was forced to resign after 37 years in power. Media reports indicated that as his number of critics grew, he became the caricature of an African dictator, who destroyed an entire country in order to keep his job. Moreover the former South African President Jacob Zuma who recently stepped down after intense pressure from his own party (ANC). Mr. Zuma, the erstwhile "people's president", was in power since 2009, with faced numerous allegations of corruption, fraud, racketeering, money-laundering and tax evasion. His case had been termed the Zombie case that was challenged, dropped, changed, challenged again and reinstated over so many years, by so many different lawyers, judges and prosecutors and he will likely continue the long legal battle to clear his name. All these cases point to the public loss of faith in the country, state owned institutions and democratic system. In Ethiopia Prime Minister Hailemariam Desalegn has resigned and a state of emergency was declared as government fears an outbreak of ethnic unrest across the country. Much of the concern in Ethiopia is the lack of democratization and as such abuses of human rights and freedom for all ethnic groups. The dilemma faced in the wake of this resignation is to hold an election that makes democracy more of a possibility and appeases everyone and not just a select few.

The question that we are tasked with is simply are these cases of civil unrest across Africa indicative of a lack of consistency in delivering to people what they want? Or is it brought about by leaders who enjoy the seat of power so much that it gets hard for them to leave peacefully? Well, I believe we can all look at it differently, depending on which side of the coin you choose to look at it from. What we really need to focus on though, is why people want change? How do we respect democratic principles; people's choices and more importantly how do we sustain this change? At the core of it one can argue that change cannot be sustained by its very definition change would have to adapt. For this reason we should be mindful of there always being something that will prompt a need for change, whether this is on account of a newer generation, higher literacy levels within countries, or a thirst for isomorphic mimicry amongst others. We should consider how we can welcome change whether by governments or people and how do we address the variety of complex factors at work in a given country?
