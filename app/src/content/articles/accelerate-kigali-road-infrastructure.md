Road infrastructure development is not just about how much money is spent. It is about delivering it efficiently, strategically, and at scale. When roads are built the right way, they unlock growth, create jobs, connect markets, and accelerate prosperity far beyond the construction phase itself. Leveraging tools like land value capture can make this transformation financially sustainable while ensuring that the benefits of public investment are reinvested into further development. A careful reading of National Strategy for Transformation 2 (NST2) and Vision 2050 makes one reality clear; Rwanda's ambitions are bold, but their realization depends on how strategically we finance and implement infrastructure. If we are serious about achieving the Rwanda envisioned for 2050, road infrastructure particularly in the City of Kigali must be accelerated in scale, quality, and financing innovation. Kigali currently has approximately 2,753 km of roads, yet only 686 km are paved. This leaves 2,067 km still unpaved. Since 2022, the government has paved 120 km of roads. Spread over roughly four years, this averages about 29 km per year. Out of 686Km 31Km were built by communities and 15Km were built by communities in partnership with the City of Kigali. Irrespective of this great initiative, those built both by communities are not sustainable due to the fact that they are not professionally built.

At that same pace, paving the remaining 2,067 km would take approximately 71 years. When financing constraints and competing national priorities are factored in, the realistic completion timeline could stretch close to eight decades. That trajectory does not align with Vision 2050. Urban expansion across Gasabo, Kicukiro, and Nyarugenge continues rapidly, yet infrastructure delivery remains constrained by fiscal capacity. At an estimated cost of around USD 1 million per kilometer in urban settings considering drainage, utility relocation, and engineering standards paving the remaining 2,067 km would require roughly USD 2.067 billion. Financing this entirely through conventional public budgets would significantly delay delivery.

This is precisely where policy innovation becomes necessary. One viable and globally tested solution is land value capture (LVC), a financing mechanism that aligns infrastructure funding with the economic value it generates. When a properly engineered road is constructed with drainage systems, sidewalks, lighting, and integrated stormwater management surrounding property values increase significantly. This appreciation is a direct result of public investment. Rather than treating infrastructure solely as a public expenditure, a structured contribution from benefiting property owners can create a sustainable financing stream.

Consider a practical example: Assume a 1 km urban road segment costing approximately 1.3 billion RWF directly benefits 400 plot owners. If each plot owner contributes 300,000 RWF per year structured as 30,000 RWF per month for 10 to 15 years, the model becomes affordable and predictable. 400 plot owners × 300,000 RWF per year = 120 million RWF annually. Over 10 years = 1.2 billion RWF. Over 15 years = 1.8 billion RWF.

Under this structure, a commercial bank, infrastructure fund, or contractor would pre-finance construction upfront. Monthly contributions would be deposited into a legally ring-fenced infrastructure account dedicated exclusively to repaying the pre-financer. Roads are built immediately; repayment occurs gradually. For property owners, this is not a burden but an investment. In areas where plots have increased from around 15 million RWF to 60 million RWF following road improvements, a total contribution of 3–4.5 million RWF over more than a decade represents a small fraction of the wealth created.

For government, the advantages are strategic: Reduced upfront fiscal pressure, accelerated construction timelines, higher engineering and durability standards, bankable infrastructure projects due to predictable repayment streams, and stronger citizen ownership and accountability.

The government would retain oversight responsibilities ensuring technical standards, drainage quality, environmental compliance, and fairness in cases requiring expropriation. But citizens would transition from passive beneficiaries to co-investors in durable infrastructure. At the current average of 29 km per year, Kigali risks waiting more than seven decades to fully pave its remaining road network. That pace is misaligned with NST2 and Vision 2050. If we aim to build a competitive, service driven urban economy, infrastructure financing must evolve. Roads are not merely transport corridors, they are economic multipliers that unlock land value, attract enterprise, and improve quality of life.
