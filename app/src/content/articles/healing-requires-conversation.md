Thirty-two years after the Genocide against the Tutsi, Rwanda has made remarkable progress in rebuilding trust, strengthening institutions, and nurturing a shared national identity. The country made a deliberate choice to focus on what unites its citizens rather than what once divided them. Emphasizing that we are Rwandans first has been central to restoring cohesion and preventing the return of narratives that once fuelled violence. Yet an important question remains: how do we ensure that younger generations understand history without feeling that the subject itself is off-limits?

Recently, I experienced a moment that brought this dilemma into sharp focus. I was travelling to Amsterdam on a KLM flight from Kigali via Entebbe, seated between a young Rwandan lady named Chantal and a Norwegian passenger. Chantal is in her twenties and studies in Italy. As is often the case with Rwandans, our conversation with Chantal quickly revealed mutual connections. We found ourselves discussing Rwanda and the Great Lakes region more broadly with the Norwegian guy.

At one point, the Norwegian passenger asked whether we were Hutu or Tutsi. The question immediately made Chantal uncomfortable. I responded calmly that we were Tutsi and explained that our families survived the genocide because they were in the diaspora at the time. Chantal shared that her parents had lived as refugees in what was then Zaire and returned to Rwanda in 1996. My grandparents returned in November 1993 and were resettled in Umutara in the areas that had been liberated by the RPF. For us, we came back in 1995 and landed in disserted Kigali.

When the gentleman briefly stepped away to help himself, Chantal turned to me and quietly asked, "Are we allowed to talk about who we are?" Her question was sincere but also revealing. It reflected how some young people may interpret the emphasis on national unity as meaning that discussions about ethnicity or history should be avoided entirely.

I explained that Rwanda's vision is clear; we are all Rwandans first. This identity is intended to transcend the categories that were once manipulated to divide society. However, choosing unity does not mean pretending that history did not happen. It means understanding the past in a way that builds responsibility, empathy and vigilance. Our children and young people need clarity, not silence.

In many families, conversations about the genocide are approached cautiously, often because of the pain associated with those memories. Others may feel hesitant because of the complex roles their parents and relatives played during that period. While such hesitation is understandable, avoiding discussion altogether can unintentionally create uncertainty among younger generations. Questions that remain unanswered rarely disappear; instead, they tend to resurface later, sometimes shaped by misinformation or incomplete narratives.

Rwanda's experience demonstrates that structured dialogue can support healing. Commemoration periods such as Kwibuka, school discussions and community dialogues have created space to reflect responsibly on history while reinforcing unity. These efforts show that acknowledging the past does not weaken national cohesion; rather, it strengthens it by ensuring that lessons are neither forgotten nor distorted.

Over the coming years, there is value in encouraging careful but honest intergenerational conversations. Young people should understand why Rwanda chose the path of unity and why rejecting divisive labels is a deliberate national commitment. At the same time, they should feel confident asking questions about history without fear that the subject itself is forbidden.

Healing is not a one time event; it is a continuous process. Silence alone cannot sustain it. Responsible conversation, grounded in respect and a shared identity, helps ensure that future generations carry forward both the lessons of the past and the commitment to remain above all Rwandans.
