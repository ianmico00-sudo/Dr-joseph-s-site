One of the most important assets Rwanda possesses is President Kagame. He is a leader who has consistently expressed a clear desire to see Rwandan citizens move out of poverty and achieve shared prosperity. From my own interactions with both Rwandan and foreign investors, it is evident that his leadership has played a decisive role in spear heading and attracting investment into the country. When foreigners asked why they chose Rwanda, many point to his insistence that systems must function and that development should translate into visible change. This kind of leadership resolve is often cited as a missing ingredient in many African contexts.

I recall a moment when President Kagame addressed government officials and cautioned that poverty cannot be hidden. While data may suggest progress, his outreach visits had revealed persistent hardship within communities. That contrast between statistics and lived reality remains an important reminder. On the 5th and 6th of February this year, I had the opportunity to attend Umushyikirano 2026. As I reviewed the recommendations that emerged from the dialogue, one issue stood out clearly: service delivery remains one of the most significant constraints to achieving our national development targets.

This observation resonated strongly with two personal experiences. The first dates back to late 2025, when I began paying closer attention to the energy sector, largely due to the frequent power outages experienced since December. Businesses and households alike have felt the impact, including losses caused by unstable electricity supply, at a time when electricity tariffs have also been adjusted upward. These challenges have made power reliability an increasingly urgent issue. Last week on Thursday, while in Rubavu, a key tourism destination, I had scheduled a meeting at 10 a.m at the Serena–Kivu Hotel. As I was sitting in the lobby, the sound of a running generator was noticeable. When I inquired why the noise, I was told that grid power was fluctuating. This was particularly concerning given the importance of the meeting, which brought together government representatives and development partners in a hybrid physical and virtual format. Such meetings require absolute reliability, and power interruptions at that level carry reputational and operational risks.

From an energy perspective, Rwanda currently operates 32 power plants with a combined capacity of 279 megawatts and approximately 676 kilometers of power lines, according to open data sources. Daily national consumption stands at around 260 megawatts, with more than 100 megawatts purchased from East Africa Power Pool (EAPP), a cross border bilateral arrangement. These figures illustrate how close the system operates to its limits, leaving little room for disruption placing us as a country in being power insecure.

Interestingly around town, there is an investor proposing a renewable energy project capable of generating over 300 megawatts per day, exceeding the current capacity of the national grid REG. For nearly a year and a half, the proposal has moved between REG, the Ministry of Infrastructure, and the Ministry of Environment. While due diligence, environmental assessments, and tariff negotiations are necessary and justified, the prolonged process contrasts sharply with the ongoing power shortages affecting citizens and businesses. Institutional concerns around grid capacity, technical skills, contractual obligations, and pricing are valid, yet the urgency of service delivery remains pressing.

The second experience dates back from 2016 until 2018, when I was involved in early discussions with the Ministry of Health by then around a proposed public private partnership to establish a small oxygen production facility. The objective was to improve access to medical oxygen for district hospitals. At the time, none of us anticipated the COVID-19 pandemic. Despite goodwill, the initiative stalled due to delays, limited follow-up, and coordination challenges. Eventually, the partners chose to pursue the investment in Kenya, where processes moved more predictably. These experiences raise a difficult but necessary question: when service delivery fails at this scale, is the challenge purely institutional, or does it also reflect our collective behaviour? Institutions do not operate in isolation. They function through people, our colleagues, relatives, and fellow citizens who are entrusted with responsibility. Ultimately, societies deliver outcomes that align with what they tolerate.

One of the most damaging bottlenecks to effective service delivery and a major reason economic transformation is delayed, is not always the absence of policy or resources, but inaction and weak accountability at the point of execution.

Perhaps the solution lies not only in structural reform, but also in moral renewal. We need partnerships that extend beyond policy frameworks, including engagement with churches, civil society organizations, media and community institutions, to reinforce values of integrity, responsibility, and working smart. Development is not solely about plans and financing, it is equally about character, ethics, and the willingness to act when duty calls. In this regard, it is encouraging that the Umushyikirano recommendations echo many of the bottlenecks that civil society organizations in Rwanda have consistently highlighted over the years.
