Last week, I had the opportunity to be invited to the National Umushyikirano, and I would like to begin by appreciating the President for clearly stressing the importance of collaboration between government, the private sector, and civil society in driving Rwanda's development agenda. This message is both timely and necessary, particularly as our challenges become more complex and increasingly interconnected.

During the discussions, I listened attentively to one of the key panels of the day, which brought together the Minister of Agriculture, the Minister of Finance, the leadership of the National Institute of Statistics of Rwanda (NISR), and a young entrepreneur. The conversation focused on how to support sustainable development and was rich, grounded in data, and refreshingly honest. It also reaffirmed that agriculture is the backbone of Rwanda's economic growth, as clearly stated in the National Strategy for Transformation (NST2).

Building on this recognition, Rwanda has made commendable progress in moving agriculture beyond subsistence. Household consumption as a share of total agricultural production has declined, meaning more produce is reaching the market. However, too much of what we produce leaves farms in raw form, while much of what we consume arrives as processed imports. We grow maize but import maize flour. We produce milk but import dairy products. We harvest crops but lose a significant share after harvesting due to weak storage systems, limited cold chains, and insufficient processing capacity.

Yet, despite the progress made and steady growth in agricultural output over the past decade, our country continues to rely heavily on imported processed foods. This apparent contradiction raises an important question. Why has rising farm output not translated into reduced food imports?

Data from the National Institute of Statistics of Rwanda illustrates this gap clearly. According to the Rwanda Statistical Yearbooks 2018 and 2025, Rwanda's total imports rose from about 2,875.99 million USD in 2017 to 6,360.45 million USD in 2024. Over the same period, exports also rose from roughly 806.15 million USD (including re-exports) to about 3,012.72 million USD. While export growth is notable, the widening gap between imports and exports shows that domestic demand is expanding faster than domestic supply, particularly in processed food products. This gap points to weaknesses in agro-processing, value addition, and post-harvest systems, rather than a failure by farmers to produce.

From my perspective, this challenge is not primarily about production. Rather, it is a value chain problem. In practice, this single gap manifests at multiple points along the system, including irrigation, land fragmentation, and financing.

One critical constraint is irrigation, as climate change disrupts rainfall patterns, irrigation has become central to food security. While irrigated land has expanded significantly in recent years, more than 400,000 hectares with irrigation potential remain undeveloped. Even where irrigation systems exist, maintenance, affordability, and after sales services remain major constraints, especially for smallholder farmers. Without reliable irrigation, production remains seasonal and unpredictable, making it difficult to sustain agro-processing operations that depend on consistent supply.

Additionally, fragmentation of production further limits progress. Most farmers operate on very small plots, which makes professional farming and reliable aggregation difficult. Agro-processors cannot function efficiently when supply is unpredictable in both quantity and quality. Even though land consolidation has helped, it must be fully leveraged and directly linked to market-oriented production and processing.

Access to finance adds another layer to these challenges. Agro-processing, irrigation, storage, and cold chains all require capital, yet affordable and long-term financing remains limited. High interest rates and risk averse lending practices discourage investment across the value chain. Without blended finance, credit guarantees, and contract-based financing models, private investors will struggle to fill the agro-processing gap.

We should not let this gap in the value chain persist. As Rwanda's population is projected to grow significantly in the coming decades, pressure on food systems will only intensify. This means productivity per hectare must rise sharply, and food systems must become more efficient and resilient. Producing more alone will not be enough if we do not transform how food is processed, stored, and distributed.

Addressing this challenge, however, cannot be the responsibility of one actor alone. As clearly emphasized in the Umushyikirano discussions, the government must continue to provide policy direction, infrastructure, and coordination. At the same time, the private sector must invest in processing, logistics, and technology, while civil society plays a critical role in supporting youth and smallholder farmers' organization, accountability, and inclusive participation.

Therefore, in my view, reducing food imports and closing this value chain gap will require a deliberate shift from a farm-first mindset to a value chain first approach. This means planning production, processing, finance, and markets together. It means treating agro-processing as a strategic pillar of food security rather than an afterthought. It also requires aligning incentives, public procurement, and investment frameworks around domestic value addition and import substitution.

Overall, Rwanda's progress in agriculture is real and deserves recognition. But if we want that progress to translate into lower food imports, more jobs, and stronger food security, the next phase of transformation must focus on closing the agro-processing and value chain gap. That is where the real opportunity lies.
