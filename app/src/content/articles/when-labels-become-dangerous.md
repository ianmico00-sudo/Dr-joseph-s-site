Yesterday the 7th of April, thirty-two years after the Genocide against the Tutsi, offered more than a moment of remembrance. It was also a moment of reflection: what have we learned, and what must continue to change so that such a tragedy is never repeated anywhere again. Why do we learn to hate? In most cases, hatred does not ignite with an act of violence; it begins with a word. It starts the moment we stop seeing our neighbors as individuals and start seeing them as categories.

Thirty-two years ago, in a span of just 100 days, more than one million lives were extinguished during the Genocide against the Tutsi. Yet, this tragedy did not materialize out of thin air in 1994. Its foundation was laid decades prior, when people were systematically labelled as different, unwanted, or dangerous.

History serves as a stern reminder that mass violence is often the harvest of small, repeated ideas. When language is used to strip dignity from a group, it creates a psychological distance between people who once shared bread and stories. Labels act as a veil, making it easy to forget that those we are taught to fear are human beings with families, dreams, and hopes identical to our own. Once a society becomes comfortable with this "othering," discrimination feels justifiable, and the path to violence is cleared.

Rwanda understands the price of division better than perhaps any nation on earth. For years, identity was weaponized to separate citizens rather than unite them. Suspicion replaced trust, and harmful narratives sowed seeds of resentment among communities that had coexisted for generations. By the time the crisis erupted, the social fabric had already been shredded by years of dehumanizing rhetoric.

However, Rwanda also stands as a testament to the possibility of healing. Over the last three decades, the nation has made deliberate, arduous efforts to rebuild trust. Initiatives like Ndi Umunyarwanda ("I am Rwandan") encourage citizens to prioritize their shared national identity over the divisions of the past. During Kwibuka (Remembrance), communities gather not just to mourn the lost, but to recommit to the protection of our collective humanity.

Today, reconciliation is woven into the mundane moments of everyday life. Former adversaries now trade in the same markets and collaborate in cooperatives, while children attend the same schools to learn a curriculum rooted in empathy and critical thinking. National programs further emphasize the weight of words and the responsibility of every citizen to guard against divisive ideology.

Hatred is neither natural nor inevitable; it is a learned behavior. Because it is learned, it can be unlearned. The enduring lesson from Rwanda is that words matter. Preventing violence requires a conscious rejection of narratives that reduce complex humans to simple, dangerous labels. If hatred is built brick by brick, then unity is built the same way. Every conversation that fosters understanding and every action that honors human dignity is a step toward ensuring that "Never Again" is not just a slogan, but a lived reality.
