Last month, the Rwanda Social Security Board (RSSB) introduced a new premium structure for the Community Based Health Insurance (CBHI) scheme, widely known as Mutuelle de Santé. This reform deserves recognition because it reflects the country's continued commitment to investing in the health sector. Health and Education are sectors that require sustained investment regardless of economic cycles. Whether a country is developing or already prosperous, these two pillars remain essential for human development, productivity, and long term national prosperity.

Over the past two decades, Rwanda has made remarkable progress in expanding access to healthcare services. Community Based Health Insurance has been one of the most transformative policies in this journey. It has enabled millions of citizens to access hospitals, health centers, and essential medical services without the fear of catastrophic health expenditures. Current estimates indicate that the government invests about $57 US dollars per person per year on healthcare services. For a developing country, this represents a significant effort. However, the reality of healthcare is that the demand for services, medicines, and infrastructure continues to grow as the population increases and life expectancy improves.

Two days ago, I was reminded of the importance of this system when a family member fell sick. As often happens in our communities, relatives and friends came to visit and express their sympathy and support. After some time at bed side, I took a walk through some of the hospital wards and had the opportunity to interact with former colleagues. Moments like these remind us that illness can affect anyone. The true strength of a health system is measured by how well it protects families from distress during such moments. I also encountered some relatives of other patients who had brought food in flasks to support their loved ones admitted in the hospital, which reflects the strong spirit of solidarity that still exists within our communities.

While the new CBHI premiums have already started being implemented, it is still useful to reflect on how contributions could be structured in a way that strengthens the sustainability of the system while remaining fair to citizens. I have been thinking about this and I reviewed recent national statistics regarding income distribution in Rwanda.

Recent data show that Rwanda's income distribution remains uneven. About 50.8% of the population are classified as low-income earners, with monthly incomes ranging from Rwf0 to Rwf200,000. Meanwhile, 46.5% fall within the middle-income category, earning between Rwf200,000 and Rwf1.2 million per month. Only 2.7% of the population belongs to the high-income group, earning above Rwf1.2 million per month. These figures underline the importance of policies that continue to expand productive employment and strengthen Rwanda's emerging middle class.

Using these income categories, one can imagine a contribution structure that reflects citizens' varying levels of ability to pay while ensuring the sustainability of the health system. If we assume Rwanda's population is approximately 14 million people, contributions could be structured in the following way.

Low-income earners, who represent about 50.8% of the population, could contribute Rwf8,500 per year. This group represents approximately 7,112,000 people. Their combined contribution would generate about Rwf60,452,000,000 annually.

Middle income earners, representing about 46.5% of the population, could contribute Rwf45,000 per year. With approximately 6,510,000 people in this category, the total contribution from this group would generate about Rwf292,950,000,000.

High income earners, representing about 2.7% of the population, could contribute Rwf85,000 per year. With around 378,000 people in this group, the total contribution would generate approximately Rwf32,130,000,000.

When these three contributions are combined, the total annual resource envelope generated for community-based health insurance would reach approximately Rwf385,532,000,000. This is roughly 385.5 billion Rwandan francs, which is equivalent to around 290 to 300 million US dollars depending on the exchange rate.

Such a resource pool could significantly strengthen the financing base of Rwanda's health system. It could support the purchase of medicines, improve health center infrastructure, strengthen referral hospitals, and ensure that medical personnel are adequately equipped to serve patients across the country.

However, it is important to acknowledge some practical realities within households. Many families in Rwanda have more than four members. When contributions are calculated on a household basis, larger families may face a heavier financial burden compared to smaller households. This is an important aspect that policymakers may continue to reflect upon to ensure that the system remains equitable and does not unintentionally disadvantage households with many dependents. Flexible arrangements or additional social protection measures could help ensure fairness across different family sizes.

In addition, even within the low-income category there are households that may still struggle to afford contributions. This is where social protection programs must continue to play a critical role. The most vulnerable households, particularly those in category one and parts of category two of the poverty classification system, should continue to receive government support or subsidies so that no citizen is excluded from healthcare because of poverty.

Rwanda has already demonstrated that targeted social protection programs can work effectively. Initiatives such as monthly cash transfers, public works programs, and other safety net mechanisms have helped protect vulnerable households from falling deeper into poverty. Integrating these mechanisms with health insurance financing can ensure both equity and sustainability.

Another aspect worth considering is the structure of how contributions are paid throughout the year. Rather than requiring households to pay the full amount at once, a monthly payment system could make the contributions more manageable for many families. For example, contributions could be paid in instalments from January to June. By the time July arrives, the majority of households would have completed their payments for the year. This approach could allow the financial year for the scheme to effectively begin in July when the contributions have already been collected.

Such an arrangement could provide greater predictability in planning for the health sector. When the government and relevant institutions know that the bulk of contributions have already been mobilized by mid-year, it becomes easier to plan procurement of medicines, allocate budgets to health facilities, and manage resources more efficiently throughout the year.

Ultimately, building a strong healthcare system requires continuous investment, careful planning, and a strong partnership between the government and citizens. Rwanda has already laid a solid foundation through policies that prioritize universal health coverage. What remains is to continue refining the financing model so that it remains fair, inclusive, and capable of meeting the growing healthcare needs of the population.

Investing in health is not a luxury. It is a necessity. A healthy population is more productive, more resilient, and better prepared to contribute to national development. As Rwanda continues its journey toward Vision 2050, strengthening the financial sustainability of community-based health insurance will remain one of the most important investments the country can make.
