I was honored to be invited to the consultative meeting held Monday this week at Gako Military Academy as a participant from civil society organization. The meeting brought together leaders from Central Government and Local Government to reflect on how to advance citizen centered governance, strengthen collaboration, and improve service delivery outcomes.

Beyond my attendance, I had the opportunity to share perspectives from the civil society space on areas where gaps continue to affect implementation of well-designed national strategies. One of the recurring challenges relates to feedback mechanisms. In many cases, some leaders remain uncomfortable receiving feedback, especially when it highlights weaknesses in implementation. Yet even strong performers require continuous feedback from beneficiaries. Policies and programs are designed to improve the lives of citizens, therefore the lived experience of citizens should remain the primary reference point for evaluating performance.

Another issue discussed relates to inter and intra government relations. Coordination challenges between institutions sometimes affect planning coherence and timely execution of government programs. When ministries, agencies, districts, and implementing institutions are not sufficiently aligned, delays emerge and citizens ultimately bear the consequences. Rwanda has demonstrated strong capacity in policy formulation, but implementation gaps still exist due to coordination challenges and differing institutional priorities.

I emphasized the importance of decentralization as a practical pathway for improving service delivery. Since 2021, a total of 522 services were planned to be decentralized. However, to date, only 232 services representing 44 percent have been decentralized. Among these, 127 services are fully decentralized while 105 remain partially decentralized. Meanwhile 290 services equivalent to 56 percent are still centralized. These figures suggest that decentralization remains a work in progress. In practice, citizens still need to travel long distances or navigate complex procedures to access services that could otherwise be delivered closer to where they live.

Rwanda has already demonstrated the positive impact of combining decentralization with digitalization through platforms such as Irembo. Through digital public services, citizens can apply for birth certificates, criminal records, driving tests, land services, and many other services without physically visiting offices. This has reduced time, transport costs, and uncertainty. It shows that when digitalization supports decentralization, efficiency increases and transparency improves. However, digitalization must continue to be accompanied by institutional readiness at local levels to respond quickly and effectively once citizens submit applications.

Partnership frameworks also require strengthening. Existing coordination platforms such as the Joint Action Development Forum were originally designed to promote collaboration between government, development partners, private sector actors, and civil society. However, in some instances, these platforms are increasingly perceived mainly as spaces for resource mobilization discussions rather than spaces for honest reflection on the performance of programs and policies. JADF can play a stronger role as a platform for evidence sharing, identifying implementation bottlenecks, and improving accountability between stakeholders.

Another important aspect raised during discussions was the need for public institutions to recognize and utilize evidence generated by non-state actors. Civil society organizations, research institutions, and universities regularly produce analysis that can complement government data and provide practical insights from communities. Rwanda already benefits from tools such as the Rwanda Governance Board Citizen Report Card and Governance Scorecard which provide structured feedback from citizens on the quality of public services. Expanding the culture of evidence informed decision making can help institutions respond more effectively to emerging challenges.

In his closing remarks, the Head of State, President Paul Kagame emphasized accountability and raised an important question to decision makers present. The question focused on why implementation gaps persist even when institutions have the mandate, resources, and policy clarity required to deliver results. The inability of participants to provide clear answers, beyond acknowledging the challenge, reflected the complexity of implementation dynamics.

From practical experience working with government institutions, it is evident that the expectations for performance are high. Leaders operate in an environment where mistakes can have serious consequences for citizens and for their professional responsibilities. In such a context, some officials may become risk averse, preferring to wait for detailed instructions before taking decisions. This can slow innovation and weaken ownership of responsibilities. When decision making becomes overly centralized or cautious, institutions may shift into reactive or emergency modes of working, which can produce short term outputs but undermine long term planning effectiveness.

Rwanda has made remarkable progress over the past three decades in rebuilding institutions, strengthening security, expanding access to education and health services, and improving economic opportunities. These achievements are strongly linked to visionary leadership and a governance culture that prioritizes citizens' welfare. Institutions, particularly in the security sector, continue to play a critical role in safeguarding national stability which remains a foundation for development progress.

However, sustaining progress requires continuous institutional learning and adaptation. Honest conversations are necessary to understand why certain implementation gaps persist despite strong policy frameworks. Addressing these challenges does not require complex theoretical solutions. Many of the answers lie in strengthening trust in local government institutions and equipping them with adequate financial resources, human capacity, and decision-making authority.

Empowering districts, sectors, and cells to deliver services effectively can significantly reduce administrative delays and improve responsiveness to citizens' needs. Central Government can then focus on policy direction, performance monitoring, and ensuring that implementation reflects national priorities while responding to realities on the ground. Strengthening data sharing between institutions, improving coordination mechanisms, and reinforcing accountability systems will also contribute to improved outcomes.

The discussions at Gako reaffirm that Rwanda possesses strong institutional foundations and committed leadership. The task ahead is to continue strengthening collaboration across government levels and between state and non-state actors. Citizen centered governance requires continuous dialogue, mutual trust, and shared responsibility.

As we move forward, the focus should remain on ensuring that policies translate into tangible improvements in citizens' daily lives. Effective decentralization supported by digitalization, stronger coordination, and evidence informed decision making will remain key pillars for achieving sustainable and inclusive development outcomes.
