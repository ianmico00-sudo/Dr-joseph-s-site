We grew up in Kyebando and in Greater Mpigi at a time when history was not something you learned from textbooks, it was something you absorbed through whispers and absences. As children, politics revealed itself through loss. You would hear that so-and-so's father had been killed during the time of Idi Amin. Then under Milton Obote, another family would quietly lose a parent. A few years after the National Resistance Movement came into power, the pattern did not entirely disappear. This time, someone's father was taken because he was associated with Federo. He was picked up and never seen again. No explanation. No trial. No closure. Just silence.

That was the political classroom of our generation. Many Ugandans who are now in their 50s and mid-50s did not simply grow up poor, they grew up orphaned by politics. Some lost parents to outright violence, others to detention, exile, or quiet disappearance. The state was not a neutral arbiter; it was often a source of fear. Trust in institutions eroded early, and survival became a private, family-level responsibility rather than a public guarantee.

It is against this background that leadership in Uganda must be understood. When President Yoweri Museveni came to power in 1986, he did not inherit a blank slate. He inherited a traumatized society, exhausted by cycles of regime change, militarized politics, and arbitrary rule. Ugandans were not merely demanding elections; they were demanding safety, voice, and predictability.

Uganda's colonial experience under the British Empire had already shaped this contradiction. Colonial administration introduced the language of democracy, law, and representation, while hollowing out local political agency. Ugandans therefore understood democracy conceptually long before they experienced it substantively. Independence raised expectations that post-colonial leaders would finally align political freedom with dignity and development.

Across Africa, however, independence exposed a structural weakness. Before independence, political movements operated as pressure groups with a single unifying goal: removing colonial rule. After independence, those same movements failed to mature into stable political institutions. Leaders fell out with peers, factions hardened, and disagreement was reinterpreted as betrayal. Opposition ceased to be an alternative government-in-waiting and instead became an irritant to be neutralized.

Uganda illustrates this trajectory vividly. The early post-independence fallout between Obote and traditional institutions, particularly the Kingdom of Buganda, culminated in the 1966 constitutional crisis. From that moment, political disagreement increasingly took military rather than institutional form. This path eventually paved the way for Amin's dictatorship, which devastated institutions, destroyed the economy, and normalized violence as a political tool.

The scars of those years were generational. Entire communities learned to keep quiet, to avoid politics, and to mistrust authority. By the time Museveni emerged from the guerrilla struggle rooted in the Luwero Triangle, the population was desperate for stability more than ideology.

One of the most consequential shifts under Museveni was the opening of political and civic space, particularly freedom of expression. Compared to earlier regimes, Ugandans could speak, organize, publish, and criticize with significantly reduced fear. Over time, this openness became embedded in everyday life. Radio talk shows flourished. Political debates moved into public spaces. Today, platforms such as NBS Frontline and NBS Barometer routinely host direct, sometimes confrontational engagement with those in power.

This level of openness matters, especially in a region where dissent has often been criminalized. Yet freedom of expression is not an end in itself; it is a means. Uganda's challenge is that free speech gradually became performative rather than productive. Criticism expanded faster than solutions. Many mastered the language of opposition without building the institutions required to govern.

Museveni has now ruled Uganda for nearly forty years. To some, this is inherently unacceptable. To others, continuity in a fragile democracy is not automatically a flaw. Young democracies require time to test policies, build institutions, and stabilize political culture. Constant leadership turnover every four, five, or seven years can disrupt long-term planning and reduce governance to short-term populism.

Under this continuity, Uganda has experienced tangible changes; expanded road networks, increased electricity generation, post-conflict recovery in northern Uganda, regional trade integration, and a consistent diplomatic footprint in East and Central Africa. These outcomes did not occur by accident; they were the result of sustained policy enforcement and institutional memory.

However, continuity also breeds comfort. For nearly four decades, many Ugandan intellectuals, activists, and politicians have operated in a predictable political environment. Instead of using that stability to build strong alternative institutions, ethical leadership pipelines, and credible policy platforms, many settled into routines defined by media visibility and rhetorical confrontation.

The opposition's weakness is therefore not merely ideological; it is structural. Too many opposition leaders are concentrated in Kampala, detached from rural constituencies where the majority of Ugandans live. Outside the capital, their presence is often symbolic. Constituency development is thin. Institutional capacity is weak. Politics becomes about microphone time rather than service delivery.

This is why the central problem in Uganda is not simply the NRM. It is the failure of alternative leadership to deliver where it governs, to model ethical conduct, and to build systems that outlive personalities. Uganda has produced strong men, but the more important question is whether it has produced smarter successors. Leadership is not only about courage or defiance; it is about institution-building, mentorship, and generational transfer of values.

Freedom of expression without responsibility can intoxicate a society. Ugandans learned to speak freely, but too often forgot to translate that freedom into prosperity, ethical leadership, and durable systems. Criticism became abundant, while delivery remained scarce.

My late father Peter Claver Nkurunziza used to say, "Time will tell." History eventually distinguishes between those who invested in noise and those who invested in substance. Uganda's story is still unfolding, but its lessons are already clear for the region and the continent. Pressure politics without institution-building is unsustainable. Freedom without responsibility stagnates. Continuity without renewal risks decay.

As Uganda approaches its elections on 15 January 2026, the most important hope is not merely peaceful voting. It is a deeper reflection. Freedom of expression must mature into freedom to build, to govern ethically, and to deliver tangible outcomes to citizens. Only then does democracy move from rhetoric to reality.

I wish Uganda peaceful elections and, more importantly, the wisdom to turn its hard-won freedoms into shared and lasting prosperity.
