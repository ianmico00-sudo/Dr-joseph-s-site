Yesterday evening gave me an opportunity to reflect on something important about Kigali's social life and economic potential. My colleague was leaving for Washington DC on a 2 a.m. flight, which meant he had to head to the airport a little after midnight. Instead of sitting at home waiting for time to pass, we decided to step out and explore some social spots around the city. Some time ago a friend had mentioned that Amahoro Stadium and BK Arena had spaces designed for clubs, restaurants, and other social venues. I remembered that in 2021, when BK Arena had just opened, a group of friends and I had actually explored the idea of renting one of those spaces. At the time the rent was extremely high, and we simply could not afford it, so we dropped the idea. Still, I am someone who is always thinking about ideas that could contribute to prosperity, so I decided to pass by the area and see what it looks like today.

To my surprise most of the spaces were quiet. The only place that seemed active was Zaria Court, and they are doing an impressive job. The place is open, welcoming and lively. We stopped there briefly and had a cocktail before moving on because we were actually looking for a club. Eventually we ended up at Tiamo, the place had real energy. It was vibrant and full of young people. What caught my attention was the profile of the crowd. Most of the people there were under 35. Many were young professionals working in finance, technology and entrepreneurship. I realized I was probably among the very few approaching 50.

Many of them recognized me and came to greet me jokingly. They said, "Dr., nobody believes you can dance these strokes, meaning going down and up." I laughed and told them that our generation lived its youth with gratitude. We were fortunate that the country invested in our education after the difficult years of rebuilding. We worked hard, we supported our families, and we carried responsibilities toward society, we have no regrets.

One young man made a remark that stayed with me the whole evening. He said, "Because of the hard work of your generation, we are living, not surviving." That statement carried a lot of meaning. The young people we see today enjoying opportunities, careers and social life are benefiting from decades of stability and development that Rwandans collectively worked for.

But while standing there and observing the energy of the youth, another question kept coming to my mind. Why are the spaces around Amahoro Stadium and BK Arena not functioning as vibrant social hubs, yet the infrastructure is already there? Last year I visited Stockholm and stayed near Strawberry Arena. What impressed me there was how the stadium is integrated into a broader ecosystem. Around the stadium there are hotels, restaurants, sports bars and shopping malls. The entire area is active almost all the time. Even when there is no match or concert, people still come to eat, shop, relax and socialize. The place operates almost around the clock.

Kigali has built impressive infrastructure at and around Amahoro Stadium and BK Arena. These are world class facilities. However, if the rent is so high that young entrepreneurs in hospitality cannot afford the spaces, then the business model may need to be reconsidered.

Some young people working in hospitality told me that managing those spaces is simply too expensive. If that is the case, perhaps the approach should be adjusted. Instead of focusing only on high rental prices, the institutions managing these facilities could consider more affordable rents. Tenants can cover their operational costs such as electricity, water and air conditioning. That way the facilities remain active while still covering operational expenses.

Beyond business, there is also a social aspect to consider. Kigali still has very few spaces where people can relax, exchange ideas and interact in a more open setting. Young people often gather in only a handful of clubs, many of which are small and crowded. There are almost no sports bars, cultural cafés or relaxed spaces where people can debate ideas, talk about policy, entrepreneurship or sports.

There is also a missing generational balance. It would be healthy to have spaces where professionals over 40 can also gather. Such environments allow younger people to interact with experienced individuals who can share insights, mentorship and life experience. Cities that grow and innovate often benefit from these informal spaces of interaction.

A vibrant night economy also contributes to employment, tourism and creative industries. Kigali already has the infrastructure to support such an ecosystem, especially around Amahoro Stadium and BK Arena. This is why the institutions responsible for managing these facilities may want to reflect on the current model. The objective should not only be maximizing rent but creating a lively ecosystem that attracts people, businesses and ideas.

What started as a simple outing while waiting for a friend's flight ended up giving me a deeper reflection about the future of Kigali's social spaces. The infrastructure already exists. What remains is to ensure that it becomes accessible and active so that these places truly come alive and serve the city and its people.
