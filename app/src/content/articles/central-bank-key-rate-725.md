The recent decision by the National Bank of Rwanda to raise the Central Bank Rate to 7.25 percent has sparked an important public conversation about inflation, financial stability, and the future of small and medium enterprises. This is a healthy and necessary debate, because monetary policy is not abstract. It directly affects how households live and how businesses operate every day. In my message to the Central Bank via X (Twitter), I acknowledged the logic behind the decision, noting that safeguarding macroeconomic stability and containing inflation are essential for the long-term health of the economy. At the same time, I raised a concern shared by many entrepreneurs. As interest rates rise, a growing number of SMEs are effectively being priced out of credit. This limits their ability to invest, expand, and create jobs. In such conditions, banks naturally find it safer to lend more to the government, which is widely seen as lower risk.

The Central Bank's response to these concerns was thoughtful and consistent with its mandate. It emphasized that macroeconomic stability and inflation control are vital not just in general, but specifically for SMEs. When inflation is left unchecked, small businesses are often the first to suffer through rising input costs, unstable demand, and reduced confidence from lenders and investors. The Bank also acknowledged the trade-off directly, explaining that the challenge of protecting stability while ensuring SMEs are not crowded out is being addressed through complementary government measures to support access to finance.

This exchange makes one thing clear. The discussion is not about opposing goals. Both sides want a stable economy, predictable prices, and a strong private sector. The real issue is how policies are sequenced and how quickly support reaches businesses on the ground.

For SMEs in places like Kigali, Huye, Musanze, or Nyagatare, higher interest rates often translate into postponed investments, tighter cash flows, and reduced working capital. At the same time, households are grappling with higher food prices, rising electricity tariffs, and increased transport costs, all of which reduce consumer spending. Stability is crucial, but liquidity is equally important for survival.

Three practical actions to protect the economy while supporting SMEs:

At the country level there should strengthen targeted and concessional financing windows for SMEs. Government-backed credit facilities can help shield productive businesses from the full impact of higher interest rates, especially in priority sectors such as agro-processing, manufacturing, health, and renewable energy. This approach allows monetary policy to fight inflation without locking viable enterprises out of credit altogether.

There is a need for coordination between fiscal and monetary policy to be more responsive. When interest rates are tightened, fiscal measures should move faster. This includes timely payment of government suppliers, temporary tax relief during tightening cycles, and clear communication on how long support measures will last. Predictability builds confidence, which is just as important as the cost of borrowing.

The private sector is at the forefront of remitting taxes monthly, quarterly and annually therefore VAT refund timelines should be reformed to ease cash-flow pressure on SMEs. The Rwanda Revenue Authority could introduce a system for quarterly VAT refunds instead of annual reimbursements. For many small businesses, delayed VAT refunds tie up working capital at the worst possible time, effectively acting as an interest-free loan to the state. Faster and more predictable refunds would immediately improve liquidity, reduce dependence on overdrafts, and support compliance and job retention.

Ultimately, the central Bank's policy challenge today is not choosing between stability and growth but making sure the two move together. The Central Bank is right to prioritize macroeconomic stability, and entrepreneurs are equally right to raise concerns about access to finance. The way forward lies in smart coordination, quicker fiscal responses, and administrative reforms that free up cash for businesses. If these complementary measures are implemented decisively and felt at the SME level, Rwanda can protect stability without squeezing the very private sector that is essential for inclusive and sustainable growth. Thank you for the continuous effort of protecting the economy.
