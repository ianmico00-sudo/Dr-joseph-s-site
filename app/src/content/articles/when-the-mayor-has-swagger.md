Why the Recent Festivities Signal a Shift in National Happiness

The fireworks have faded and the streets are quiet once more. This calm after celebration brings an unavoidable return to reality. The work of building the society we desire does not pause for holidays.

On one of those festive nights, I moved through Kigali on the back of a motorcycle to hop from one club to another. It was a choice for immersion rather than convenience. The city vibrated with music, laughter, and movement from Kimihurura to Kiyovu and Remera to Nyarutarama. Windows were down, terraces were full, and the air carried a sense of collective release. Kigali felt alive in a way that was difficult to ignore. It did not feel like a city rushing through the holidays to get back to serious business.

The 2025 end-of-year festive season in Kigali was truly remarkable. Social venues, cultural spaces, hotels, and restaurants remained active long after sunset from 24 December through 4 January 2026. Tourists and regional visitors filled terraces in Kimihurura, Nyamirambo, Gahanga, and Kiyovu. Youth-driven cultural spaces in Kimironko and Giporoso pulsed with music, poetry, and community performances until the early hours. Major concerts and parades drew thousands. BK Arena hosted headline shows featuring influential artists such as Shenseea, Mavado, Bruce Melodie, The Ben, Ariel Wayz, Juno Kizigenza, alongside gospel artists like Israel Mbonyi. These acts reinforced the growing cultural pull of the city.

Rwanda has ranked low on the World Happiness Index for years and consistently trails behind the global average. The happiness score for Rwanda stood at 3.16 on a ten-point scale in 2023. This was a decline from 2022 and sat significantly below many countries at comparable income levels. The score has fallen from earlier highs above 3.5 over the past decade. These figures reflect enduring challenges in how citizens perceive their quality of life. The 2025 festive season invites a deeper reflection on national well-being against this backdrop. The atmosphere in Kigali felt distinctly different this year. Many Rwandans previously travelled to Kampala, Nairobi, Bujumbura, Mombasa, or Zanzibar in search of festive energy. Kigali itself became the destination this time. Friends from Europe who once joked that the city felt like a quiet rural town during the holidays were surprised to find it buzzing with life and social confidence.

I am often labelled as overly critical when it comes to societal issues. That posture is rooted in historical understanding rather than cynicism. Historian Alison Des Forges references a Rwandan proverb in "Defeat Is the Only Bad News" that captures a deep-seated dynamic in our political culture. She suggests that defeat is the only bad news that matters. This insight explains how public discourse became high-stakes and intolerant of dissent under historical pressures. Such a legacy helps explain why critical civic engagement has not always been encouraged and why open dialogue remains a work in progress.

Critique and appreciation are not opposites. No one can meaningfully appreciate success without the ability to analyze it critically. Celebrating the success of the festive season should go hand in hand with reflecting on what it reveals about citizen well-being and institutional responsiveness. Government and local leadership play a central role in shaping these outcomes. Social transformation at scale does not occur by accident. It is enabled by leadership that provides structure, safety, and opportunity. Security services were visible and responsive during the festive period. Emergency health teams were on standby, traffic management was effective, and businesses had the space to operate. The result was an environment where people felt safe to gather and participate fully in public life.

These conditions matter because they shape how citizens feel about their lives. Public satisfaction grows when people feel safe and part of a shared national moment. Cultural life demonstrated its power to enrich everyday well-being through family gatherings at the Kigali Convention Center and large-scale concerts at Camp Kigali (KCEV). The economic implications are equally important. A vibrant night-time economy generates value across hospitality, transport, and creative industries. Strong returns in revenue, job creation, and city branding are likely if stakeholders analyze the cost-benefit outcomes of the season.

Leadership style matters as well. The energy and coordination demonstrated by the City of Kigali and the National Police showed what proactive governance can achieve. The lesson is clear as the country looks toward local government elections in November 2026. Leadership that prioritizes citizen experience is a necessity. The 2025 festive season was more than a series of events. It was a collective expression of civic pride and social resilience. The lived experience of this season shows that Rwanda has the capacity to improve citizen well-being through well-organized public life. Future happiness reports may finally begin to reflect the progress many citizens already feel if this spirit is sustained beyond the holidays.

Happy New Year. We can only hope that the joy and connection witnessed this month will continue to shape a year where ambition is matched by collective happiness.
